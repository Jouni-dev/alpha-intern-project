The Lighthouse at Merrow Point

Elena Voss had kept the light at Merrow Point for six years, ever since the district office ran out of reasons to send a younger keeper to a rock that flooded twice a year. She liked it that way. The tower was forty-two feet of gray stone, built in 1911, and its lamp could be seen eleven miles out on a clear night. On the night this story begins, nothing was clear. A storm had come up fast from the southeast, and Elena had spent the evening checking the lamp mechanism by lantern light because the generator kept surging.

At half past eleven, she heard something over the wind that wasn't wind — a scraping, metallic sound from the rocks below the tower. She took the storm lantern down the outside stairs, bracing herself against the rail, and found a man face-down in the tide pool at the base of the lighthouse, one arm still hooked through a length of broken rigging. She dragged him above the waterline and got him inside.

He didn't wake until the following afternoon. He gave his name as Tomas Rigg, twenty-nine years old, the last survivor of a four-person crew aboard a fishing boat called the Kestrel Anne. Since he'd been unconscious for most of the wreck, he wasn't fully sure what had gone wrong; he remembered the mast cracking, and after that only cold water. Around his neck, on a cord, he had been wearing a brass compass, badly dented on one side, with two initials scratched into the underside of the lid: M.H.

Elena recognized the make of the compass immediately — it was a naval-issue piece, the same kind her grandfather Henrik Voss had kept at Merrow Point when he was keeper, decades before her. Henrik had vanished during what was logged as a routine supply run in the autumn of 1975, and no wreckage from his boat was ever recovered. Elena had grown up hearing two versions of the story: one where he'd been swept out and drowned, and one, whispered by the older fishermen in town, where he'd simply given up the job and walked off somewhere no one from Merrow Point would ever find him. Elena had never fully believed either version.

Tomas stayed at the lighthouse for four days while the district boat was delayed by weather. In that time he told Elena why he had been at sea at all, despite having no fishing experience: he was looking for his father, Mattias Holt, a merchant sailor who had gone missing off this same stretch of coast twelve years earlier, in 1975. Tomas had spent years collecting fragments of the story — a distress call logged by a passing tanker, a secondhand account from a retired harbor pilot — and all of it pointed toward the waters near Merrow Point. He had taken a berth on the fishing boat specifically because its route passed close to the lighthouse. The compass, he explained, was the only thing of his father's he had ever been given; his mother had received it in the mail two years after Mattias disappeared, with no letter and no return address, only a postmark from a mail station on the mainland side of the strait.

On the third day, while repairing a section of loose flooring in the lamp room, Elena's crowbar caught on something that wasn't floorboard. Underneath, wedged into a gap in the stone, was a logbook wrapped in oilcloth, its cover stamped with her grandfather's initials, H.V. She had never known it existed. Most of the entries were routine — weather readings, supply notes, the small complaints of a man alone with a light. But the last six pages were different. Henrik had recorded, in increasingly cramped handwriting, that he'd pulled a half-drowned sailor from the rocks in the autumn of 1975 — a man he named only as Holt — nursed him back to health over eleven days, and eventually rowed him across to the mainland himself rather than wait for the supply boat, because Holt had begged him not to report the rescue to the shipping company he'd been fleeing. The final entry, dated the night before Henrik's own disappearance, said only that he had given Holt his spare compass "so the man would have something of the lighthouse to carry with him," and that he intended to tell no one what he'd done.

Elena read the entry three times before she understood what she was holding. She brought the logbook down to Tomas that evening and set it in front of him without a word. He read the last six pages standing up, and did not sit down again until he had finished them a second time.

Neither of them ever learned why Mattias Holt sent the compass back to his family two years after Henrik rowed him ashore, or where he had gone in the years between, or whether he was still alive. The logbook said nothing about that, and Henrik was not there to ask. What the logbook did make clear was that the compass Tomas had worn around his neck into the wreck, the same one that had washed up with him on the rocks below Merrow Point, had once belonged to Elena's grandfather — and that some version of the story the old fishermen told, about Henrik simply walking away, had a grain of truth buried inside it after all.

The district boat finally reached Merrow Point on the fifth day. Tomas left with the logbook's last six pages copied out by hand into his own notebook, and with a promise to write once he'd tracked down whatever record existed of a mail station on the mainland side of the strait. Elena kept the original logbook in the lamp room, in the gap in the floor where she'd found it, and told no one at the district office that she had it.

The lamp at Merrow Point still turns every night, on the same eleven-mile reach it always has. The dent in the compass, the one Tomas had worn since he was a boy, was never explained, and Elena never asked. Some things, she had decided, were better left as the sea gave them: partly told, and never quite finished.
